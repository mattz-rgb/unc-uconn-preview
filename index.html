The setup

This is a must-win for North Carolina. The Tar Heels are a touchdown-plus favorite at UConn on Saturday, and if they want to finish with six or seven wins, they have to be 5-4 when Louisville visits Chapel Hill.

UNC (4-4, 2-3 ACC) has lost back-to-back one-score games at Kenan Stadium, 31-30 in overtime to Syracuse and 38-35 to No. 1 Miami, and it needs two wins from its last four to reach a bowl. UConn (4-4, independent) is coming off a 35-26 loss at Air Force, where it had the ball for 18:06 and gave up 366 rushing yards on 74 carries.

Carolina is favored by anywhere from 7 to 8.5, depending on the book and the day. The line opened at 8.5, dropped to 7.5 and has the total at 52.5, with UNC at -298 on the moneyline and UConn at +240.

Win-probability models give the Tar Heels 80.8 percent. Kickoff is noon ET on CBS Sports Network, and the forecast is 61 degrees, so weather is not a factor.

The history is short. This is the fifth meeting and UNC leads the series 3-1, but the one loss is the one people remember. UConn beat Carolina 27-14 in the 2024 Fenway Bowl, a game in which the Tar Heels didn't pick up a first down until 24 seconds were left in the first half. The Tar Heels' last trip to East Hartford was in 2009, a 12-10 win. Almost nobody from either bowl roster is still around.

The Huskies' roster is built on nearly 60 transfers. UNC's quarterback has beaten them before, too: Billy Edwards Jr. opened the 2024 season at Maryland by going 20 of 27 for 311 yards and two touchdowns against UConn.

The math is what makes it a must-win. At 4-4, Carolina has four games left: UConn, Louisville at home, Virginia on the road and NC State at home. Six wins makes a team bowl eligible, and seven would be a real step for a program that went 4-8 a year ago. Win Saturday and the Tar Heels are 5-4, one win from six with three games left and two from seven. Lose and they are 4-5, which means they have to win two of the last three just to get to six, and seven takes a sweep that ends with beating an NC State team that has won five straight in the series.

This is also the last non-conference game and, by the market's pricing, the easiest of the four. If Carolina finishes 1-3, this is the game it will wish it had back.

UConn's math is simpler. A win Saturday would leave the Huskies one home win from six, with James Madison and Old Dominion visiting Rentschler Field the next two weeks. Both teams have something to play for, but only one of them is playing a game it can't afford to lose.

With that little room for error, the details matter. That's where the rest of this goes.

The two teams

These teams have opposite problems. UNC's offense is efficient in the spots that decide close games, and its defense is what has let it down late. UConn's offense is the more explosive one, and its defense has been run over, picked apart or both in most of its losses. The numbers below run through Oct. 31, so they include Carolina's Miami game and UConn's Air Force game.

North Carolina. Bill Belichick is 4-4 in his second season after going 4-8 in his first, and the roster looks nothing like the one he inherited. Edwards is a veteran transfer quarterback, tight end Jelani Thurman plays almost every snap, and sophomore tackle Xavier Lewis has been the best pass-rush story in the ACC.

Bobby Petrino replaced Freddie Kitchens as offensive coordinator, and the offense has been described as veer-and-shoot. In practice that means quick, coverage-based reads, a lot of tight end usage and 59.8 plays a game, a slow-ish pace. Edwards after the Pitt win: "Every play is designed to be an 80-yard touchdown."

The defense is a multiple 4-2-5 that has lost Steve Belichick, who went on medical leave Aug. 6 and resigned effective Oct. 27 after a university investigation the school considers resolved. Brian Belichick has been calling plays. General manager Michael Lombardi resigned Sept. 3. None of it has shown up in the game plan in an obvious way, but the staff has dealt with turnover at the top of the defense all fall.

Here is how the season has gone. The Tar Heels beat TCU 15-10 in Dublin, rolled East Tennessee State 35-3 and blew a 17-6 halftime lead at Clemson.

They hung with No. 3 Notre Dame for a half before losing 37-26, won at No. 25 Pitt 21-17 for Belichick's first ranked win, won at Duke 24-20 after leading 17-0 and watching Duke score 20 straight, and then lost in overtime to a Syracuse team that came in on an 11-game ACC losing streak.

They are 3-1 on the road or at neutral sites and 1-3 at home. They are also 0-2 against top-five teams, with one of those losses decided by a fumble in the final six minutes. You can read that as a team that competes with anybody or a team that can't close, and the record supports both.

UConn. Jason Candle spent 15 years at Toledo, won 74 games there, went 11-3 with an 8-0 MAC record in 2023 and signed a six-year deal through 2031 after Jim Mora left for Colorado State.

The roster is Candle's Toledo roster with other pieces added. Quarterback Kalieb Osborne, running back Kenji Christian, linebacker K'Von Sherman and defensive tackle Esean Carter all followed him from Toledo. The transfer haul includes running backs Cyncir Bowers (West Virginia) and Trey Cornist (Central Michigan), linebacker John Lista (Penn) and cornerback Zion Paret (Florida Atlantic).

Offensive coordinator Nunzio Campanile, formerly Syracuse's quarterbacks coach, has said the offense will emphasize balance, physicality and running the football. Defensive coordinator Ryan Manalac came from Pitt and runs a 4-3.

The Huskies have played two different seasons. In four wins they have scored 56, 48, 34 and 35 and allowed 15.8 a game. In four losses they have scored 14, 21, 41 and 26 and allowed 38, 24, 42 and 35, an average of 34.8.

The Maryland loss (38-14) is what happens when UConn's offense is stopped and its defense is tested through the air. The Miami (OH) loss (24-21) came after a 14-0 lead. Syracuse needed overtime to win 42-41 after UConn rallied in the fourth quarter. Air Force was an option-offense outlier more than a verdict on the defense.

The one common opponent is Syracuse, and it's a useful comparison. The Orange beat UNC 31-30 in overtime at Kenan Stadium, gaining 455 yards on 83 plays, 199 on the ground. They beat UConn 42-41 in overtime at Rentschler Field with 509 yards on 85 plays, and Amari Odom threw for 355 yards and five touchdowns. Both teams lost in overtime. Syracuse scored 11 more points against the Huskies and got more yards through the air.

The efficiency numbers split. UConn's offense is better than UNC's in points, yards per play, yards per carry and yards per pass attempt. UNC's defense is better than UConn's in points, yards per play and yards per pass attempt allowed, but UConn's defense is the better one against the run and on third down. UNC is better on offense on third down and in the red zone.

UConn's 6.81 yards per play is inflated by a 671-yard day against Lafayette. Without that game it's 6.4. UNC's schedule has been far tougher, with two top-five teams, a ranked Pitt and an ACC slate. UConn is 0-2 against FBS teams with winning records, and its four wins came over FCS Lafayette, Southern Miss, Temple and UMass.

Position group breakdowns

Each group covers North Carolina first, then UConn, then a verdict. Stats run through Oct. 31 unless noted. This is a non-conference game, so there is no ACC availability report, and neither team has released a depth chart for Saturday. Who starts at left tackle for UNC, whether UConn's Javon Brown is fully healthy after a concussion and who plays in the Tar Heels' secondary won't be clear until the coaches talk and the teams warm up.

Quarterbacks

North Carolina: Billy Edwards Jr. (graduate student, 6-3, 220). Edwards is coming off the best game of his Carolina career, going 28 of 34 for 341 yards and four touchdowns against Miami. It earned an 87.7 offensive grade, a season high.

For the year he is 149 of 235 (63.4 percent) for 1,872 yards, 15 touchdowns and five interceptions, and he is excellent with time and ordinary without it. Against Miami he was 25 of 29 for 318 yards and three touchdowns from a clean pocket and 3 of 5 for 23 yards under pressure.

His strip-sack with 5:33 left was Carolina's only turnover of the game, and it decided the game. He is not a runner (43 carries for 10 yards, sacks included), so the offense lives or dies on his protection and his reads.

The interceptions cluster. Two came against Notre Dame, one on an overthrown deep ball to Jordan Shipp. Two came at Pitt, one of them a third-and-short prayer to Shipp. One came at Clemson in a 13-of-30, 135-yard game that remains the low point of the season.

He hasn't thrown one in his last three games, against Duke, Syracuse and Miami, and he has started using the middle of the field. Four of his five attempts to the intermediate middle against Miami were completed for 54 yards, and three of those completions were touchdowns.

Carolina used play-action on only 10 of his 41 dropbacks against Miami, and he was 7 of 9 for 95 yards and a score on them. The staff should be looking at that this week.

UConn: Kalieb Osborne (junior, 6-4, 218). Osborne followed Jason Candle from Toledo, where he spent two years learning the system and started the Boca Raton Bowl against Louisville. Candle named him the starter on the eve of the opener.

He is 129 of 215 (60 percent) for 1,857 yards, 14 touchdowns and two interceptions, a better touchdown-to-interception ratio than Edwards has. He has thrown for at least 214 yards in six of eight games, including 314 against Syracuse and 263 in the Air Force loss.

He runs, too: 8 carries for 90 yards and two touchdowns against Lafayette, 13 carries and a score at Miami (OH). Campanile has called him about as gifted a thrower as he has been around, and the tape backs that up on the deep ball and the off-platform throw.

The problem is the protection. Against Syracuse, Osborne faced 22 pressures, was hit four times as he threw and was sacked three times. Against Maryland he went down five times in a game in which UConn managed 223 total yards.

Candle said after the Air Force game that the line isn't protecting his quarterback well enough and that he is taking too many hits. Jake Merklinger, a Tennessee transfer, is the backup.

Edge: slight lean to UNC. Edwards takes better care of the ball. Osborne is the better athlete and the more dangerous player once a play breaks down. The pass rush decides which of those matters on Saturday.

Running backs

North Carolina: a three-back rotation with no workhorse. Carolina has carried 237 times in eight games, fewer than 30 a game, for 864 yards and 10 touchdowns, a 3.65 average.

The run game complements Petrino's passing offense and doesn't drive it. Benjamin Hall leads the team with 296 yards on 60 carries (4.93 a pop, no touchdowns), Demon June has 289 on 72 with a team-high eight scores, and Jaylen McGill has 246 on 47 at 5.23 with two.

Hall is the best pure runner. He had 18 carries for 130 yards at Clemson, but a hamstring has had him in and out. He left the Duke game in the first quarter and came back against Miami for 14 snaps.

June is the touchdown back, the first Tar Heel with a rushing touchdown in four straight games since Omarion Hampton in 2023, and he scored on a three-yard pass in overtime against Syracuse.

McGill has grown the most. He carried the load at Duke after Hall went down, finishing with 12 carries for 75 yards and the 24-yard, go-ahead score with 1:53 left. Against Miami he caught six passes for 69 yards and graded 87.8 as a receiver.

The trouble is the bottom end. Notre Dame held UNC to 77 yards on 23 carries, and Syracuse outrushed the Tar Heels 199-101. The offensive line hasn't generated consistent movement against good fronts, and Edwards can't make up the difference with his legs.

UConn: the strength of the roster. The Huskies lost 1,240-yard rusher Cam Edwards to Michigan State and rebuilt the room anyway. They average 5.32 yards a carry, with 1,526 yards on 287 attempts and 21 rushing touchdowns, and they get there with three backs who do different things.

Cyncir Bowers, a sophomore from West Virginia, is the lead back: 163 yards on 16 carries against Syracuse, 84 at Southern Miss, 131 on 15 against UMass and 84 on 18 at Temple. Trey Cornist, a 6-0, 215-pound Central Michigan transfer, is the physical one. He ran for 114 yards on seven carries at Temple, including a 51-yarder.

Senior Kenji Christian, who followed Candle from Toledo, is the home-run threat. He scored on his only carry at Temple from 39 yards and on a 46-yard burst at Air Force, but a hamstring injury kept him out of the Syracuse game. Dashun Reeder and Jaden Hart are in reserve, and Osborne adds designed runs.

The wins tell the story. UConn ran for 213 yards at Southern Miss, 248 at Temple and 265 against UMass and won those games by 28, 14 and 19 points.

In the Maryland loss it ran 31 times for 67 yards and never got going. At Air Force it ran only 21 times for 105 yards, but the Falcons held the ball for 41:54 and UConn trailed most of the way, so that number doesn't mean much.

Carolina
UConn

Edge: even. UConn's backs are deeper, faster and more productive, at 5.32 yards a carry against 3.65, and they are best positioned to hurt a Tar Heel defense that Notre Dame and Syracuse ran on. What holds this at even is the other side of the ball. Carolina's defensive line is the most disruptive front in the game, and a back is only as good as the lane he gets.

Receivers and tight ends

North Carolina: the best unit on the offense, and it runs through the tight ends. Jordan Shipp is the lead receiver, with 45 catches for 587 yards and three touchdowns (13.0 a catch). He had a career-best 109 yards against Notre Dame, 93 at Pitt and a 16-yard touchdown against Miami.

He also fumbled at Duke on a play Duke returned for a touchdown, and he was the target on several of Edwards's worst throws, so the connection can go either way.

Thurman, the 6-6, 233-pound tight end from Ohio State, has 38 catches for 461 yards and a team-best six touchdowns. He played 65 of 67 snaps against Miami and earned an 89.0 receiving grade for five catches, 70 yards and two scores. Belichick said afterward that Thurman is big and fast, a big target and hard to overthrow, with a great radius and the ability to make plays after the catch.

The tight ends are the feature of this offense. Thurman and Shamar Easter (9 catches, 156 yards, two touchdowns, a 17.3-yard average) account for 47 of the team's 152 receptions, 617 of its 1,894 passing yards and eight of Edwards's 15 touchdowns. Easter is the vertical threat, with a 75-yard seam touchdown at Pitt that turned that game.

Behind Shipp, the outside group is a set of complementary pieces. Trech Kekahuna, a 5-10 Wisconsin transfer, has 17 catches for 219 yards, caught the 15-yard touchdown that made it 38-35 and doubles as the punt returner. Mason Humphrey (6-4, 215) adds length when healthy, true freshman C.J. Sadler has eight catches for 114 yards, and Jaden Jefferson has a 98-yard kickoff-return touchdown on his résumé.

Edwards threw to nine different receivers against Miami. Belichick's description of the system is that the quarterback throws to whoever the coverage leaves open.

UConn: a deep, fast group without a go-to guy. Redshirt sophomore Shamar Porter (6-2, 205) leads the team with 16 catches for 308 yards and three touchdowns, which says how evenly the targets are spread. He has been the most reliable piece early, with touchdowns against Maryland and at Southern Miss, and he usually has a long catch.

Javon Brown, a 6-4, 198-pound redshirt freshman from Toledo, caught six passes for 92 yards and a score against Lafayette but missed the Temple game with a concussion. Ryder Treadway (6-3, 193) had 57 yards against Syracuse, 63 at Temple and a diving two-yard touchdown at Air Force. Ky "Flash" Wilson, a 5-9 Youngstown State transfer, made the catch that put UConn in the red zone at Air Force, and J.D. Willoughby had a touchdown against UMass.

Tight end Alex Godavitarne, a 6-4, 235-pound Division II transfer from Wheeling, had a 55-yard touchdown at Air Force on his first catch since Week 3. Matt Ragan, a Boston College transfer, is the other tight end.

The passing game works because Osborne can get the ball to players in space and the receivers will win contested balls. Syracuse's 22 pressures forced him to throw into tight windows, and he hit several of them. This group makes plays more than it creates mismatches.

Carolina
UConn

Edge: UNC. The Tar Heels are bigger, more productive and more reliable, and the size gap is real. UConn's cornerback rotation includes 5-9 players, and Carolina can line up the 6-6 Thurman, 6-4 Humphrey and Shipp against them. UConn is deeper and faster, but it has no receiver who commands a defense's attention the way Thurman does.

Offensive lines

North Carolina: functional, thin and one tackle spot to worry about. The Tar Heels' line has been the most-scrambled group on the roster. Left tackle Jordan Hall had appeared in only four games before Miami, and his absences forced Andrew Threatt out to tackle and true freshman Cristian Alvarez into the lineup at guard.

Against Miami, Hall played all 67 snaps, graded a season-low 54.8 (33.8 as a pass blocker) and was charged with five of the 11 pressures UNC allowed (one sack, one hit, three hurries). Threatt, a Charleston Southern transfer now at left guard, was better in pass protection (72.4) but was flagged for the holding penalty that wiped out a 12-yard Benjamin Hall touchdown run.

Center Christo Kelly, a captain, graded 65.3 and played every snap. Alvarez, the true freshman, is the right guard at 63.9 on 55 snaps, and Aidan Banfield rotates in at left guard. Right tackle Jac'Qawn McRoy, a 6-8, 370-pound Arkansas transfer, was the best of the group, with a 76.0 pass-blocking grade against Miami.

Some context for those numbers: one sack and 11 pressures against the No. 1 team in the country is a reasonable night. A run game averaging 3.65 yards a carry is not.

Belichick said after the Notre Dame game that you always need a backup plan on the line, because it's rare in college or the pros to keep the same five together all year. Hall's health and his play at left tackle is the biggest variable on this side of the ball against UConn's edge rushers.

UConn: a transfer-built line that is giving up too much. UConn's line is a patchwork of transfers and young players coached by John Morookian, formerly of Michigan, and it has been the least consistent group on the team.

Osborne was sacked five times against Maryland, three times against Syracuse (22 pressures), twice at Miami (OH) with three more hurries and once more on the failed two-point try at Air Force.

The line is also undisciplined. UConn has been flagged 68 times for 620 yards, 8.5 penalties a game, which is double North Carolina's rate, and three of the six penalties at Miami (OH) were false starts and holding calls on the line. Fourth-and-6 at Air Force, when Osborne was pressured on an incompletion, is a play this line hasn't been able to win.

The run game is better. UConn averages 5.32 yards a carry and has scored 21 rushing touchdowns, and Bowers, Cornist and Christian are all getting to the second level. Pass blocking and run blocking are separate problems, and the Tar Heels have the personnel to go after the first one.

Carolina
UConn

Edge: UNC, slightly. Both lines have real flaws, and Carolina's run blocking is the weaker part of its résumé. The tilt comes from the matchup on the other side of the ball, and from UConn's line giving away 8.5 penalties a game: UNC's rushers, built around Xavier Lewis and Melkart Abou Jaoude, are a bigger threat to UConn's line than UConn's rushers, led by linebacker K'Von Sherman and his 3.5 sacks, are to UNC's.

Defensive lines

North Carolina: the strength of the defense, with a caveat about the last five games. The Tar Heels' pass rush is real. Sophomore tackle Xavier Lewis, a 6-0, 250-pound former walk-on, has seven sacks and at least 10 tackles for loss. He was among the country's sack leaders early, with two in each of the first three games, including seven tackles, 4.5 tackles for loss and two sacks against TCU.

The caveat is that the production has tapered. Going by the available game logs, Lewis has one sack since the first three games. Against Miami he played 49 snaps with four tackles, no pressures and a season-low 55.0 grade. Offenses are planning around him.

That's where the rest of the line comes in. Melkart Abou Jaoude, who had 10.5 sacks last season, hurt his hand and wrist against TCU and missed two games, but he was UNC's best pass rusher against Miami with three pressures and a 70.7 pass-rush grade on 53 snaps. Jaylen Harvey, a Penn State transfer, is the other starting end.

Donovan Hoilette Jr., a Richmond transfer, had a sack at Pitt on a third down that preceded a three-and-out and graded a season-high 76.4 against Miami on 22 snaps. Leroy Jackson (37 tackles through six games) and Nicco Maggio provide interior depth, and Isaiah Johnson has been in and out with injury. Peyton Seelmann's blitzing from the second level (a sack in consecutive games) gives the front another way to win.

The weakness is the run. This group is undersized in the middle, and the tackling behind it has not been good.

Notre Dame averaged 5.4 yards on 45 carries and ran for 166 in the second half, Syracuse got 199 yards on 49 attempts, and Pitt held the ball for 36:32. Overall, Carolina allows 3.98 a carry, which isn't bad, but the games in which the front got moved are the games the Tar Heels lost or nearly lost.

UConn: veterans from the portal, a decent run front and a modest pass rush. Defensive coordinator Ryan Manalac's front is built from transfers. Seniors Esean Carter (6-2, 285, Toledo) and Tahjae Mullix (6-0, 282, Oregon State) anchor the interior, with Melvin Hills III (6-3, 302, Texas) in the rotation.

The edge group includes Anas Luqman (6-4, 260, Ohio), Joshua Mickens (Ohio State), Zakaih Saez (Oregon State), Odera Orizu (NC State) and Avery Dunn (Toledo). K'Von Sherman, a linebacker who plays like an edge defender, leads the team with 3.5 sacks, which says where the pressure comes from.

The run defense is better than the Air Force game made it look. UConn allows 3.73 yards a carry for the season. Take out the 74 carries for 366 yards that Air Force's option attack produced, and the rest of the schedule has gained 753 yards on 226 carries, or 3.33 a carry.

Air Force ran 74 times and UConn couldn't get off the field, which says little about the front. The pass rush is another matter. Mickens recorded the first sack of his UConn career earlier this season, there are no big sack totals on the defensive line, and the defense has allowed 13 touchdown passes and a 65 percent completion rate.

Carolina
UConn

Edge: UNC. Carolina's front has the more dangerous pass rushers and the more proven interior disruptor, and UConn's line has not shown it can consistently pressure a quarterback. UConn's front is better against the run than its reputation after Air Force.

Linebackers

North Carolina: two tacklers and a coverage question. In the Tar Heels' 4-2-5, two linebackers do most of the work, and both are transfers. Derek McDonald, a 6-4, 230-pound senior who spent his career at Syracuse, leads the team with 82 tackles, had 16 against his old team and 11 against Miami, and has had 10 or more tackles in six games. He is a captain and the center of the defense.

He also had his worst game of the year against Miami. He graded out at 50.1 overall and 42.3 in coverage, though he still produced five stops and a 64.3 run-defense grade. After the Syracuse loss he said the defense could have tackled better and had given up too much leaky yardage, and the coverage numbers back that up.

Peyton Seelmann, a junior from Richmond, is the weakside linebacker and the better blitzer. He had a season-high 16 tackles at Duke, a sack against Syracuse that forced overtime and another against Miami.

The depth behind them is young: Jonathan Agumadu, Timir Hickman-Collins and freshmen Calvin Thomas and DQ Forkpa.

Carolina's linebackers are productive, but with only two in the base defense and nickel or dime looks common, they are often the ones left in space against backs and tight ends. That showed up in the Miami film, where 165 of Darian Mensah's 274 passing yards came after the catch and the Tar Heels had six missed tackles.

UConn: three athletic linebackers who disrupt. The Huskies play a 4-3 with three linebackers who all have a case as the best player on the defense. K'Von Sherman, a 6-2, 236-pound senior from Toledo, is the most disruptive. He had 94 tackles, 4.5 sacks and 14.5 tackles for loss last season, has 3.5 sacks this year, and had 12 tackles, a sack and two forced fumbles against Syracuse.

John Lista, a 6-2, 220-pound Penn transfer, leads the team with 58 tackles and had 15 against Air Force and eight at Temple. Hayden Pegg, a 6-3, 200-pound junior who had been the leading tackler early, has several eight-tackle games and a sack during Air Force's two-minute drill.

The group has been solid against the run, but it is built on pursuit and pressure, and coverage is the question. Opposing quarterbacks have completed 65 percent against UConn, and Syracuse's Amari Odom threw for 355 yards. The middle of the field is where Thurman and Easter live.

Carolina
UConn

Edge: UNC. McDonald and Seelmann are the more productive pair, and the coverage matchup is what swings it. Thurman, at 6-6, against linebackers built for pursuit and a defense that has let opposing quarterbacks complete 65 percent is a mismatch this offense is designed to create. Sherman is the best individual linebacker in the game and UConn's group is better in pursuit, so this is the edge I hold least firmly.

Secondaries

North Carolina: young, banged-up and better than Miami made it look. On the season, Carolina's pass defense is solid. Opponents are completing 65.3 percent (177 of 271) for 6.6 yards an attempt with 11 touchdowns against five interceptions.

Miami was the worst game of the year by the grades, with a season-low 52.5 coverage grade. Mensah went 22 of 29 for 274 and three touchdowns, 165 of those yards came after the catch, and the Tar Heels had six missed tackles. But Miami's offense is among the best in the country, and the breakdowns were mostly tackling.

The personnel is in flux. Tre Miller, a junior cornerback who was named ACC defensive back of the week after a four-tackle, two-breakup, one-interception game at Pitt, has not played since and was ruled out against Miami. Kaleb Cost, the senior nickel, was hurt at Pitt, missed Duke and returned against Miami, where he had the highest grade of any defensive regular at 71.4. Safety Peyton Waters has been unavailable since the Duke game and was questionable last week.

That leaves a lot of work for young players, and the young players have been good. True freshman Kenton Dopson III intercepted Duke's Walker Eget at the 15 with 14 seconds left to clinch the win and had seven tackles against Miami, and freshman safety Javion Butts graded a season-high 77.1 on 26 snaps with the defense's only pass breakup.

Sophomore safety Greg Smith III has 39 tackles through six games and an interception at Pitt but was beaten for a 31-yard touchdown by Miami's Mark Fletcher Jr. Jaiden Patterson, a junior corner, had the roughest night, with a 47.1 grade and two touchdowns allowed on five catches, plus a questionable pass-interference call in the end zone that set up another Miami score.

The group doesn't give up many big plays overall, but it gets stretched when it has to cover and tackle for 60 snaps. Syracuse hit a 76-yard completion over the top and several long completions late.

UConn: a transfer rotation that gets picked apart. The Huskies' secondary has been the weakest part of the roster. Opponents have completed 65 percent against it for 7.2 yards an attempt with 13 touchdowns and four interceptions.

In three of the four losses the opposing quarterback threw for 300 or more yards: Maryland's Malik Washington (34 of 41, 347), Miami (OH)'s David McComb (19 of 29, 312) and Syracuse's Amari Odom (27 of 37, 355 and five touchdowns, with Umari Hatcher catching 12 for 152 and three scores). Temple's Ajani Sheppard hit 19 of 25 for 260 in a game UConn won comfortably.

The personnel is Toledo and portal veterans plus some freshmen. Zion Paret, a 5-9, 165-pound Florida Atlantic transfer, had 12 tackles against Maryland and is a leading tackler among the defensive backs. The rotation also includes Braedyn Moore (6-2, 204, Toledo), Ty McKinstry (5-9, Toledo), Michael Robinson III (6-0, Southern Miss), Jayden Davis (Fresno State) and safeties Jayden Price and Tracy Revels.

The group has produced four interceptions all year, two of them in the Southern Miss rout. It doesn't take the ball away, and it is undersized on the outside.

Carolina
UConn

Edge: UNC, modestly. Carolina's numbers are better, its freshmen have been better than expected, and the coverage matchup of Thurman, Easter and Shipp against UConn's smaller corners and stretched linebackers favors the Tar Heels. The qualifier is health. If Miller and Waters are both out again, Carolina is playing freshmen at three spots against Osborne's deep shots.

Special teams

North Carolina: dangerous returners, an adequate kicker and a unit that has hurt itself. Rece Verhoff returned for a fifth season after a court ruling and is the main kicker. He went 19 of 23 last year with a long of 57. This year the Tar Heels are 8 of 11 on field goals (72.7 percent) and 24 of 25 on extra points, with Aeron Burrell, an LSU transfer, in the mix and listed as questionable earlier this month after leaving the Duke game.

The punting unit averages 42.2 yards on 25 punts, a respectable figure, but it has been sloppy. A botched fourth-down punt at Duke was part of why Belichick went for it on fourth-and-4 with 3:48 left in that game. Against Miami, Carolina recovered two onside kicks in the last two minutes and had both wiped out by penalties, an offside on the first and early contact by Seelmann on the second.

The return game is the plus. Jaden Jefferson's 98-yard kickoff-return touchdown against Notre Dame gave Carolina a rare special-teams touchdown, and the Tar Heels average 29.7 yards on six kickoff returns. Trech Kekahuna is the punt returner (9 returns for 68 yards as a team).

Coverage has been fine: opponents average 18.9 yards on kickoff returns and 9.1 on punt returns. The Tar Heels have also been aggressive on fourth down, converting 8 of 11.

UConn: a strong punter, an unreliable kicking game. The Huskies are 4 of 7 on field goals (57.1 percent) and 35 of 37 on extra points, and freshman Jack O'Connor missed the extra point at Air Force that forced a two-point try that failed. Candle's postgame summary was that the unit was sloppy, that UConn gave up a fake punt and missed an extra point.

The return game has produced a punt-return touchdown (10 returns, 9.7 a return) and 22.5 yards a kickoff return. Special teams coordinator Pat Cashmore came from Washington State.

Where UNC should target

1

Osborne's protection, early and often.

This is the biggest opening on the field. UConn's line allowed five sacks against Maryland, 22 pressures and three sacks against Syracuse, and two sacks and three hurries at Miami (OH), and it still hasn't figured out how to keep its quarterback upright.

Carolina controls the game by making UConn third-and-long. That starts with Lewis and Abou Jaoude winning on first and second down, then Seelmann or a nickel blitz on obvious passing downs.

The Huskies' passing game is dangerous when Osborne has time, as it was at Syracuse for stretches. It is a lot easier to slow down when the pocket is collapsing. The catch is that Osborne can run, so the rush lanes have to stay disciplined, and a spy on third down is worth a look.

2

The middle of the field, through the tight ends.

UConn has allowed a 65 percent completion rate, 7.2 yards an attempt and 13 touchdown passes, and in three of its four losses the opposing quarterback threw for more than 300 yards. Edwards did his best work against Miami between the hashes, and three of his four intermediate-middle completions went for touchdowns.

Thurman is 6-6 and Easter averages 17.3 a catch, and the Huskies have to cover them with linebackers built to chase. Carolina used play-action on just 10 of 41 dropbacks against Miami and was 7 of 9 for 95 yards and a touchdown when it did. Expect more of it Saturday.

3

Win the size battle on the outside.

UConn's rotation includes corners listed at 5-9. Shipp has been the top receiver since Week 1, Mason Humphrey is 6-4 and true freshman Sadler has been making plays. That gives Edwards a better window than he had on the deep shots that went wrong against Notre Dame and Pitt.

4

Go for it on fourth down and finish in the red zone.

UNC has converted 8 of 11 fourth-down tries (72.7 percent) and has scored on 27 of 29 red-zone trips (93.1 percent). UConn's opponents have converted 57.1 percent of their fourth downs (12 of 21).

Carolina's kicking isn't reliable enough to settle for field goals, and Belichick has shown he'll go for it, as he did at Duke and against Miami. On the edge of field-goal range, the numbers say take a shot at the end zone.

5

Let UConn beat itself with penalties.

UConn is flagged 8.5 times a game for 77.5 yards. UNC is flagged 4.3 times for 32.4. That shows up in drive-extending first downs (UConn has handed opponents 21 first downs by penalty, UNC has handed over eight) and in field position.

Carolina doesn't have to do anything exotic to cash that in beyond keeping its own flags down. The holding call on Threatt against Miami and the two nullified onside recoveries are how a discipline advantage gets wiped out.

6

Take the ball away.

UNC has eight takeaways in eight games, which is too few for a defense that rushes the passer this well. UConn's offense has given it up seven times, and Osborne's interception at Miami (OH) came on an errant third-and-10 throw after a first-down sack.

The same pressure that produces sacks produces bad throws, and one takeaway on a short field is worth more than a drive.

Where UNC should be concerned

1

The raw numbers don't show a seven-point gap.

This is the least comfortable fact in the matchup. Strip out the FCS games (Carolina's 35-3 win over East Tennessee State, UConn's 56-7 win over Lafayette) and UNC has scored 24.4 points a game against FBS teams while allowing 25.9. UConn has scored 31.3 and allowed 27.9.

Cross-average those and you get UConn 28.6, UNC 26.1, which is a toss-up that leans the wrong way.

The case for Carolina as a seven-point favorite rests on the schedule, and it's a strong case. The Tar Heels have played No. 1 Miami, No. 3 Notre Dame, Clemson, Pitt and Duke, while UConn's FBS opponents include Maryland (2-6), Temple and Miami (OH), and the Huskies have no win over an FBS team with a winning record. But the line assumes UNC plays to its schedule-adjusted level, and the Tar Heels have lost three straight at Kenan Stadium.

2

Tackling in space against a team that lives on big plays.

UConn averages 6.81 yards a play and 5.32 a carry, and its touchdowns come in bunches: a 51-yard Cornist run, 46- and 39-yard Christian scores, a 55-yard Godavitarne catch, Osborne's scrambles.

Carolina has been missing tackles (six against Miami, 165 of 274 passing yards after the catch) and got moved by Notre Dame and Syracuse. If the front seven gets stuck on blocks and Cornist or Christian gets to the second level, a 215-pound back with a head of steam is how a 14-point game turns into a 28-point one.

3

Finishing.

UNC has let a lead get away in four games and lost three of them. At Clemson it led 17-6 at the half and lost 28-20. At Duke it led 17-0, managed 52 yards in the second half before the winning drive and needed a final-minute interception.

Against Syracuse it led 10-0, was tied 23-23 after allowing 13 points in the fourth quarter, and lost in overtime. Against Miami it led 28-24 and gave up 14 in the fourth, including the touchdown that followed the strip-sack.

The second half is the common thread. The offense goes quiet (89 yards in the second half against Notre Dame) and the defense wears down after long afternoons. UConn scored 27 unanswered points to force overtime at Syracuse and rallied from 15 down at Air Force, so there isn't much room for a late lapse.

4

Turnovers and ball security.

Carolina is minus-3 in turnover margin: five interceptions thrown, six fumbles lost and eight takeaways. The fumbles come from everywhere.

Edwards's strip-sack at Miami, Shipp's fumble returned for a touchdown at Duke and McGill's fumble in Duke territory all turned games. UConn is even on turnovers. Give this offense a short field and it can finish.

5

Left tackle and a thin secondary.

Hall's 54.8 grade and his five of 11 pressures allowed matter against a UConn front that gets after quarterbacks with linebackers and edge players. With Miller out and Waters uncertain, Carolina is leaning on true freshmen in the secondary against a quarterback who averages 8.7 yards a pass attempt and has a deep ball.

6

The hangover, and the weight of the game.

Two straight one-score losses, one in overtime and one decided by a turnover and two nullified onside recoveries, is a rough thing to carry into a road trip. Edwards said this isn't the business for moral victories, and the Tar Heels are 3-1 on the road or at neutral sites. They also know what's riding on this one, and a team that has lost two straight close games can play tight when the stakes are obvious.

Still, a noon kickoff at a motivated 4-4 home team, after a Saturday night on a national stage, is a classic letdown spot, and UConn has been in position to win every game it has lost except Maryland.

The numbers I'd watch. UNC wins comfortably if it holds UConn under 150 rushing yards, sacks Osborne at least twice and finishes with a turnover margin of zero or better.

What the bad version looks like. UConn scores on its first two drives, Osborne hits a few deep shots, Edwards throws an early interception and the Tar Heels spend the second half chasing.

That's a realistic outcome, not a remote one. It's how the Maryland game went for UConn, which was outgained 475-223, and roughly how Notre Dame went for UNC, when two interceptions fed a 21-point Irish run. If it happens here, Carolina is 4-5 and playing for survival.

To be straight about it, 31-17 is the optimistic end of where this could land. The market has this at seven to 8.5 points, the models give UNC roughly a 4-in-5 chance to win, and a 14-point margin requires Carolina to win the turnover battle and finish games, two things it hasn't done consistently.

The raw season averages, adjusted for the FCS games, say this is closer to a coin flip than the spread suggests. The argument for Carolina is the schedule it has played and the specific matchups: a pass rush against a leaky line, tight ends against a secondary that has been picked apart, and a team that stays disciplined against one that doesn't.

My most likely scores land in the one-score to two-score range, with UNC winning more often than not and 31-17 sitting among the better outcomes, not the median.

What would change the call: a clean bill of health for Tre Miller and Peyton Waters and a confirmed starter at left tackle would push me toward 31-17 or better. A late scratch of Jordan Hall or Abou Jaoude, or a UConn team that rushes for 200-plus, would move it toward 27-24. With no official injury report for a non-conference game, those statuses may not be clear until warmups.

What to watch from the opening kickoff is simple. If UNC is getting home on Osborne and UConn is punting on its third drive, the game plays out the way this breakdown says. If UConn's first two possessions are touchdowns, it doesn't.
